How do we describe motion?

A destination does not tell us when an object was elsewhere. To describe its trip, record position together with time.

First, choose what the numbers mean

Track one marked point on an object. Choose zero on a straight track and call the rightward direction positive. Position tells us how many metres the point is from zero; a clock supplies the time in seconds. This point model ignores rotation and bending.

A position–time graph puts time across and position up. Each plotted point pairs a time with a position. The graph is a record of the trip, not a drawing of the track.

The interactive comparison requires JavaScript. As time passes, the graph moves right. Increasing position makes it rise, constant position makes it horizontal, and decreasing position makes it descend—even though the physical track is horizontal throughout.

What the connecting lines assume

The straight connections are our interpolation model, not extra measurements. Equal endpoint positions alone cannot prove the object paused: it might have gone away and come back between measurements. The animation shows the chosen model.

Try a different case

An object moves left at a steady rate, then stops. Sketch its position–time graph. Does the graph ever run backward in time?

Compare your reasoning

First draw a descending line toward the right: position decreases as time increases. Then draw a horizontal line toward the right: position stays constant while time keeps increasing. The graph never doubles back along its time axis.
