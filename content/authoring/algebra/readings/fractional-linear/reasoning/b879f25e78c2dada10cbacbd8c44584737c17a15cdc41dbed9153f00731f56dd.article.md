# How do you choose the next move?

We want a number whose half, plus three, is seven. Rather than guess the number, work backward from what happened to it: first it was halved, then three was added. Removing that last addition will leave its half on its own.

Why subtract three from both sides? Imagine any number that makes the original equation true. The two sides then have equal values. Subtracting three from each leaves equal values again. We can also reverse this move by adding three to both sides, so the new equation has exactly the same solutions. We change how the condition is written without changing which number satisfies it.

Follow the same unknown through those changes. Look for the operation being undone, and for the matching change on the other side.

Subtracting first was a convenient choice, not the only legal one. We could double both sides at the start, but then we must double the entire left side, including the added three. That would give x plus six equal to fourteen. Subtracting six would still give eight. The first route exposes the half more directly; both routes preserve the solutions.

The tempting wrong move is to double just the fraction while doubling the right side, leaving the three untouched. That gives x plus three equal to fourteen, whose solution eleven fails the original condition: half of eleven plus three is eight and a half. “Do the same to both sides” means act on each whole side, not whichever term currently attracts attention.

Try a changed case before reading on: a number is divided by three, then two is added, giving six. What would you undo first, and why must the other side change too?

Subtracting two from both sides leaves a third of the number equal to four. Multiplying both sides by three gives twelve. Each move is reversible, so it preserves the solutions. Checking the original condition confirms that a third of twelve plus two is six.

Choosing a move involves two questions: will it preserve the solutions, and will it make the unknown easier to uncover? Undoing the last operation often answers both. A different order can work too, provided we change whole sides consistently.
