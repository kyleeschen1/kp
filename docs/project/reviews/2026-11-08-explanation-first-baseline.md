# What counts as one thing?

Why can we combine two groups without knowing what is inside them? And when we open the combined group, why must the count apply to both parts? These are two ways of organizing the same contributions.

We will use x and y for two real numbers whose values stay fixed throughout the explanation. You only need the idea that multiplying a quantity by a positive whole number means adding that many copies of it. The numbers inside a group may be positive, negative or zero; the count tells us how many copies we have.

Look at the expression. The first term counts three copies of the quantity inside the parentheses; the second counts two copies. A parenthesized expression can be treated as one quantity even before we calculate it. We are not claiming that x and y are the same number. We are noticing that the entire sum is repeated, with the same x and the same y in each occurrence.

Imagine writing out the additions that multiplication abbreviates: three copies of that whole, followed by another two copies. Regrouping those additions gives five copies of exactly the same whole. This is the reason the counts can combine. The parentheses tell us what counts as one copy; they do not require us to work out its value first.

Now gather the two counts together while keeping their common group intact. The move will put three plus two inside a new pair of parentheses, answering “how many copies?” The original parentheses will still answer “copies of what?” Nothing is being added to x or y. This move is often called factoring: writing a sum of products as one product by making the shared factor explicit. Here, the shared factor is an entire sum.

Evaluating three plus two will give five copies of the original group. To look inside, ask a different question: how many contributions of x are there, and how many of y? Each copy contains both. Before moving on, predict whether five groups contain five x contributions and one y contribution, or five of each. Explain your choice by following what every copy contains.

Opening the group will expose five contributions of each part. We can collect all the x contributions together and all the y contributions together because regrouping and reordering real-number addition preserves the total. That is why the five multiplies both parts. Multiplying only x would lose four contributions of y. Opening a product across a sum is called distributing; it exposes the parts that factoring had packaged together.

For a numerical check, let x be 2 and y be 4. One group is then 6. Three such groups contribute 18 and two contribute 12, for a total of 30. In the opened expression, five copies of 2 contribute 10 and five copies of 4 contribute 20: again 30. Five copies of 2 plus only one copy of 4 would give 14. This check catches the missing contributions; the argument about copies explains why the equality holds beyond these particular numbers.

There is a condition hiding in the phrase “the same whole.” If one term counts a group containing x and y, while the other counts a group containing x and z, we cannot generally add the counts and keep just the first group. For example, let x be 1, y be 2 and z be 4. Three copies of the first group and two of the second total 19; five copies of the first group total 15. The groups could happen to be equal for particular values, but their written forms do not guarantee it.

Nothing in our argument divided by the group. If x is 2 and y is negative 2, each whole is zero: five copies total zero, and the opened contributions cancel as well. A quantity does not have to be nonzero to be counted or distributed.

Try a changed case before looking back: two copies of a group containing a and b are added to four copies of that same group. How many copies of each part will the opened expression contain? What would stop you from combining the counts if the second group contained a and c instead?

Your reasoning should identify the repeated whole first. Two copies and four copies give six copies, and every copy contains both a and b, so there are six contributions of each. With different groups, the shared-whole argument no longer applies without additional information. You can still look for shared parts, but that is a different regrouping.

The useful habit is to ask both questions: what am I treating as one thing, and what must remain when I look inside it? Factoring and distributing change the organization of an expression. They preserve its value by preserving every contribution.
